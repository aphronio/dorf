# Human first

Sources: Don Norman, The Design of Everyday Things; Mitchell Hashimoto, [Feel It](https://mitchellh.com/writing/feel-it).

Design from the person's work and mental model. Human first, not tech first.

- Protect attention. Every visible element must help the person do or understand something. As AI
  does more, people have less time; interrupt only when it matters.
- Encode meaning in the interaction, not in labels, hints or instructions that explain an unclear
  design. The interface should teach by use.
- Familiar beats clever: platform-native controls, common patterns (messaging apps, standard
  states), existing industry words.
- Plain, descriptive names. If a name needs explaining, it is the wrong name.
- Low friction: fewer choices, fewer taps, autofocus where intent is clear, smooth transitions, no
  choice paralysis.
- Keep internals out of the product: no raw JSON, tool names or system strings in front of users.
- How it feels is part of the requirement.
- Copy: short, simple, the customer's own words, value first, no AI tells. Copy the owner will
  publish is in their voice (read their past posts): the shortest version first, and one pick.

Test: What does this element help the person do, and could the interaction say it more directly?
